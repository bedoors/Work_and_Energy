External forces change a system

Table 7.1

You exert a force on an object in the system, in the direction of its displacement. Afterward, the system can do something it could not do before.

Compare

A force along the object’s displacement does positive work on the system, and the system’s energy increases.

Ug gravitational potential energy: elevation above Earth

K kinetic energy: motion

Us elastic potential energy: stretch

Uint internal energy: temperature and structure

Positive, negative, and zero work

Table 7.2 and Eq. (7.1)

The sign of the work depends on the angle θ between the force and the displacement. Change the angle and watch cos θ change sign.

Direction of your force

W = Fd cos θ(7.1)

F and d are magnitudes, always positive; cos θ sets the sign. Work is a scalar. Unit: 1 J = 1 N·m.

Holding a suitcase still does zero work on it, yet you get tired: your muscles keep contracting and relaxing.

Which angle goes into W = Fd cos θ?

Exercise 7.1

Use the angle between the force and the displacement. The slope of the hill is a different angle.

θ is measured from the direction of d to the direction of F, wherever they point.

Changing the slope changes neither angle between them, so it does not change the work.

Is the energy of an isolated system constant?

Table 7.3

A toy car rolls down a frictionless track and flies off the table. Where should you release it so that it always lands at the same spot, whatever the slope?

System: car + Earth, isolated (no work done on it). To land at the same spot, the car must leave the track with the same speed, so with the same kinetic energy.

Ugi = Kf, so the same release height yi, whatever the slope.

We assume Earth does not move and the track is frictionless.

If it is hard to calculate the work that an object does on the system, include that object in the system. Then describe the process with changes in different forms of energy.

Gravitational potential energy

Eq. (7.4) and Figure 7.3

A rope lifts a box at a small, constant speed. The work done by the rope equals the change in the gravitational potential energy of the box–Earth system.

Zero level of Ug

The system

Ug = mgy(7.4)

y is measured from the zero level you choose. The values of Ug depend on that choice; the change in Ug does not.

A single object has no Ug. Without Earth in the system, Earth does work instead.

Kinetic energy

Eq. (7.5) and Figure 7.4

Your hand pushes a cart with a constant force along a frictionless track. The work you do equals the change in the cart’s kinetic energy.

Newton’s second law, F = ma, and kinematics, d = vf2 − vi22a, give

Fd = ½mvf2 − ½mvi2

K = ½mv2(7.5)

Speed depends on the observer, so kinetic energy does too.

Two fan carts, the same force

Table 7.5

Identical fans push two carts with the same force over the same distance. Cart 2 has twice the mass of cart 1. Which quantities end up the same?

The same: force, work Fd, and the change in kinetic energy.

Larger for cart 2: time, momentum, and impulse.

Smaller for cart 2: acceleration and final speed.

Hooke’s law

Table 7.6 and Eq. (7.6)

A scale pulls on two springs with the same force. The thicker, stiffer spring stretches less. For each spring, the force is proportional to the stretch.

A negative force pushes: the springs compress.

FScale on Spring x = kx

FS on O x = −kx(7.6)

k is the slope of the graph, in N/m: the stiffness. x is the stretch, not the length of the spring.

Elastic potential energy

Eq. (7.7) and Example 7.4

The force you need grows as the spring stretches. The work you do is the area under the force–stretch graph, and it becomes elastic potential energy.

Us = ½kx2(7.7)

The force grows from 0 to kx, so the average force is ½kx, and the work is ½kx · x. Like every energy, it is measured in joules.

Friction warms both surfaces

Figure 7.10 and Eq. (7.8)

A rope pulls a box across a rough carpet at constant velocity. The box and the carpet both get warmer. Which choice of system makes the energy balance?

The system

ΔUint = +fkd(7.8)

With both surfaces in the system, friction is an internal force. The increase in internal energy is shared by the box and the carpet.

Momentum and energy in collisions

Tables 7.7 and 7.8

A 1.0-kg pendulum bob hits a 4.0-kg cart at 10 m/s. Momentum is constant in every collision. Is kinetic energy?

Elastic: momentum and kinetic energy both constant. The objects do not stick.

Inelastic: momentum constant; kinetic energy decreases and internal energy increases.

Totally inelastic: an inelastic collision in which the objects stick together.

Spiderman saves Mary Jane

Example 7.10

Spiderman swings down 10 m, grabs Mary Jane, and they swing up together. Will they reach the terrace 5 m above her platform?

Swings: use energy. Earth’s force keeps changing direction relative to the motion, so impulse is hard to find.

The grab: use momentum. Internal energy increases by an amount we cannot predict.

Power

Section 7.8 and Example 7.11

Power is the rate at which energy is converted. Walking and running up the same stairs give the same energy change, at different rates.

Plot

P = |ΔE/Δt|(7.9)

P = |W/Δt|(7.10)

1 W = 1 J/s and 1 hp = 746 W. A cyclist in good shape converts about 400–500 W.

Gravitational potential energy far from Earth

Eq. (7.11)

Far from Earth the gravitational force gets weaker, and Ug = mgy stops being accurate. With the zero level at infinity, Ug is negative.

Lift 1000 kg of supplies to

Ug = −GmEmOrE-O(7.11)

rE-O is measured from Earth’s center. Pulling an object away does positive work, and Ug rises toward zero at infinity.
